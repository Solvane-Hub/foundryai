# 1. Purpose

## 1.1 Overview

The **Knowledge Pack Authoring Guide** defines the official methodology for creating, validating, maintaining, and publishing Knowledge Packs within the FoundryAI platform.

A Knowledge Pack is not simply a collection of documents. It is a structured, evidence-grounded representation of the regulatory, legal, administrative, and business requirements applicable to founders operating within a specific jurisdiction.

Knowledge Packs provide the factual foundation upon which every AI capability in FoundryAI depends. The Coordinator Agent, Compliance Agent, Funding Agent, Action Plan Generator, Nova Assistant, Trust Layer, and future specialist agents all rely on Knowledge Packs as their authoritative source of information.

Because every AI-generated recommendation ultimately derives from a Knowledge Pack, the quality of a Knowledge Pack directly determines the quality, trustworthiness, and legal defensibility of the platform's outputs.

## 1.2 Objective

The objective of this guide is to ensure that every Knowledge Pack is produced using a consistent, repeatable, and auditable process regardless of: country · territory · jurisdiction · industry · language · researcher · AI model · date of creation.

A Knowledge Pack created today should follow exactly the same methodology as one created several years from now. Consistency is a core design requirement rather than a preference.

## 1.3 Scope

This guide governs every stage of the Knowledge Pack lifecycle, including: source discovery · source qualification · authority assessment · document acquisition · document validation · metadata assignment · document versioning · knowledge extraction · chunk generation · citation creation · trust assignment · publication · maintenance · retirement.

No Knowledge Pack may be published unless it satisfies the requirements defined within this guide.

## 1.4 Relationship to the Architecture

This document is part of the FoundryAI AI Architecture series.

**Subordinate to:** Constitution · Trust Layer Specification · AI System Architecture.

**Referenced by:** Coordinator Agent Specification · Specialist Agent Contract · Knowledge Acquisition Pipeline · Knowledge Validation Standard · Knowledge Chunking Standard · Knowledge Metadata Standard · Country Expansion Playbook.

Where conflicts arise, higher-order architectural documents take precedence.

## 1.5 Design Philosophy

Knowledge Packs exist to represent reality as accurately as possible. They do not attempt to provide legal advice. They do not speculate. They do not infer requirements that cannot be supported by evidence. They do not hide uncertainty.

Instead, they expose evidence, confidence, provenance, and limitations so that both the AI system and the founder can understand exactly what is known, how it is known, and where uncertainty remains.

## 1.6 Guiding Principles

**Truth Before Fluency.** Information must never be invented to create a smoother user experience. If information cannot be verified, uncertainty must be represented explicitly.

**Evidence Before Opinion.** Every regulatory requirement must be traceable to an authoritative source. No requirement exists without supporting evidence.

**Reproducibility.** An independent researcher following this guide should be capable of producing substantially the same Knowledge Pack from the same authoritative sources.

**Transparency.** Every statement generated by the platform must be explainable through documented evidence. Users should never be asked to trust the system blindly.

**Maintainability.** Knowledge Packs are living assets. They must be designed so they can evolve as legislation, regulations, and administrative practices change.

**Scalability.** The methodology must support expansion from one jurisdiction to many without requiring changes to the underlying process. Country-specific knowledge should be expressed through data rather than custom workflows.

## 1.7 Success Criteria

A Knowledge Pack is considered complete only when it can support the generation of accurate, evidence-grounded outputs for every supported business type within its defined jurisdiction while satisfying the Trust Layer requirements established by FoundryAI.

Completeness is evaluated through measurable retrieval performance, citation quality, evidence strength, and coverage confidence rather than by document count alone.

# 2. Core Concepts and Terminology

## 2.1 Purpose

A consistent vocabulary is essential for a knowledge-driven platform. Many terms used throughout FoundryAI appear similar but represent fundamentally different concepts. Ambiguous terminology leads to inconsistent implementation, unreliable AI behaviour, and architectural drift.

This section establishes the canonical definitions used throughout the platform. These definitions are binding across all architecture documents, engineering specifications, AI agents, Knowledge Packs, evaluation procedures, and future jurisdictions.

## 2.2 Knowledge Pack

A **Knowledge Pack** is the complete, structured representation of regulatory, administrative, compliance, funding, and business knowledge for a specific jurisdiction and scope.

A Knowledge Pack consists of far more than source documents. It includes: collected source documents · structured metadata · extracted requirements · knowledge domains · document relationships · citations · version history · trust information · retrieval indexes · evaluation results.

A Knowledge Pack is the unit of publication, maintenance, and versioning within FoundryAI.

**Examples:** Bahamas National Knowledge Pack · Bahamas Tourism Knowledge Pack · Jamaica National Knowledge Pack · Barbados Construction Knowledge Pack.

## 2.3 Knowledge Document

A **Knowledge Document** is an individual authoritative source that contributes information to a Knowledge Pack.

Examples include: Acts of Parliament · Regulations · Statutory Instruments · Government Gazette publications · Ministry guidance · Regulatory manuals · Official application forms · Government web pages · Official fee schedules.

Each Knowledge Document is independently versioned and retains its original provenance. Knowledge Documents are never modified by FoundryAI. They are preserved as evidence.

## 2.4 Knowledge Chunk

A **Knowledge Chunk** is the smallest retrievable unit of information used during AI retrieval.

Chunks exist solely to optimise retrieval and should never alter the meaning of the original source.

Every chunk must maintain: source document reference · page or section reference where available · citation identifier · version identifier · trust metadata · embedding representation.

Chunks inherit authority from their parent document but possess their own retrieval metadata.

## 2.5 Requirement

A **Requirement** is a discrete obligation, permission, restriction, deadline, eligibility criterion, or procedural step that applies to a founder.

Examples include: Register with the Registrar General · Obtain a Food Handler's Permit · File VAT returns quarterly · Renew a business licence annually · Submit an environmental impact assessment.

Requirements are the primary outputs consumed by downstream AI agents. Every Requirement must be supported by at least one authoritative citation.

## 2.6 Citation

A **Citation** is the explicit connection between a generated statement and the evidence supporting it.

Every citation identifies: originating document · relevant section · page or clause where applicable · publication version · retrieval timestamp · evidence strength.

A citation allows any generated recommendation to be independently verified. No requirement may exist without a citation.

## 2.7 Evidence

Evidence is the collection of authoritative materials supporting a claim. Evidence may consist of one document, multiple documents, or multiple passages within the same document.

Evidence is evaluated according to the Trust Layer's **Evidence Strength** dimension. Evidence should always support the exact claim being made rather than merely discussing the same topic.

## 2.8 Knowledge Domain

A **Knowledge Domain** is a logical category of business knowledge representing a regulatory area.

Examples include: Business Registration · Licensing · Taxation · Employment · Immigration · Food Safety · Environmental Compliance · Health & Safety · Financial Reporting · Tourism Operations.

Knowledge Domains define the expected scope of retrieval and are used to measure **Coverage Confidence**.

Knowledge Domains are owned by Knowledge Pack metadata and consumed by the Coordinator Agent.

## 2.9 Coverage

Coverage describes how completely a Knowledge Pack addresses the domains expected for a particular business. Coverage is measured across the required Knowledge Domains rather than by document count.

For example, a restaurant may require coverage across: Business Registration · Food Safety · Employment · VAT · Environmental Health · Fire Safety.

Missing one domain reduces Coverage Confidence even if all retrieved requirements are individually correct.

## 2.10 Ground Truth

Ground Truth is the independently validated set of requirements against which FoundryAI's outputs are evaluated.

Ground Truth is created through authoritative legal review, regulatory expertise, and documented source verification. **Ground Truth is never generated by AI.**

It serves as the benchmark for measuring: Recall · Precision · Coverage · Citation accuracy · Trust performance.

## 2.11 Trust Dimensions

Every generated claim is evaluated using the four Trust Layer dimensions:

- **Source Authority** — How authoritative is the originating source?
- **Evidence Strength** — How directly does the evidence support the generated claim?
- **Reasoning Confidence** — How confident is the platform that the reasoning process correctly interpreted the evidence?
- **Coverage Confidence** — How complete is the retrieved information relative to the expected Knowledge Domains?

These dimensions are independent and must never be combined into a single numerical score.

## 2.12 Knowledge Lifecycle

```
Source Identified → Source Qualified → Document Acquired → Validated →
Metadata Assigned → Chunked → Embedded → Published → Retrieved →
Evaluated → Maintained → Retired
```

Knowledge may move backwards in the lifecycle when legislation changes or quality issues are discovered.

## 2.13 Canonical Definitions

- A **Knowledge Pack** contains many Knowledge Documents.
- A **Knowledge Document** contains many Knowledge Chunks.
- A **Knowledge Chunk** supports one or more Requirements.
- Every Requirement references one or more Citations.
- Every Citation references one or more Evidence sources.
- Knowledge Domains define expected retrieval scope.
- Coverage measures completeness across Knowledge Domains.
- Ground Truth exists independently of AI-generated outputs.

These definitions are canonical and apply across all FoundryAI systems.

# 3. Knowledge Pack Architecture

## 3.1 Purpose

A Knowledge Pack is a structured knowledge system rather than a document repository. Its architecture is designed to ensure that regulatory knowledge can be acquired consistently, validated objectively, retrieved efficiently, cited transparently, maintained over time, and evaluated quantitatively.

Every Knowledge Pack follows the same architectural model regardless of jurisdiction, language, or regulatory complexity. Country-specific differences are expressed through content and metadata rather than changes to the architecture itself.

## 3.2 Architectural Principles

**Evidence First.** All knowledge originates from authoritative external sources. No knowledge originates from AI. AI may organise knowledge but never invent it.

**Immutable Sources.** Original source documents are immutable. FoundryAI never edits legislation, regulations, or government publications. Corrections occur through versioning rather than modification.

**Layered Knowledge.**

```
Government Source → Knowledge Document → Knowledge Chunk →
Requirement → Launch Plan → Founder Guidance
```

Each layer has a different purpose and should never be confused with another.

**Separation of Content and Interpretation.** Knowledge Packs store facts. AI agents generate interpretations. Interpretation is transient; knowledge is persistent. This separation ensures improvements to AI reasoning never require rebuilding the Knowledge Pack itself.

**Traceability.**

```
Founder Recommendation → Requirement → Citation →
Knowledge Chunk → Knowledge Document → Government Source
```

Every link in this chain must remain intact.

## 3.3 Core Components

**Component 1 — Source Registry.** Records every authoritative source considered during authoring: authority · jurisdiction · publication type · publication frequency · ownership · acquisition method · current status. Provides provenance before any documents are collected.

**Component 2 — Document Repository.** Stores every acquired source document: Acts · Regulations · Gazette notices · Government forms · Ministry guidance · Licensing manuals · Official fee schedules. Each document receives a permanent identifier and complete version history.

**Component 3 — Knowledge Repository.** The structured interpretation of the document collection: requirements · metadata · relationships · classifications · citations · domain mappings. Optimised for AI consumption.

**Component 4 — Retrieval Index.** Enables efficient semantic search: embeddings · retrieval metadata · document relationships · chunk identifiers · ranking information. Exists solely to support retrieval and should never become the authoritative source of knowledge. Embeddings may be regenerated without changing the Knowledge Pack itself.

**Component 5 — Evaluation Repository.** Stored separately from operational knowledge: ground truth datasets · benchmark questions · recall results · precision measurements · coverage measurements · historical evaluations. **Operational AI must never learn directly from evaluation data.**

**Component 6 — Publication Metadata.** Every published Knowledge Pack records: version · publication date · jurisdiction · supported industries · supported languages · supported business types · trust baseline · evaluation status · maintenance owner. Enables reproducibility and auditing.

## 3.4 Knowledge Relationships

```
Government Source
        ▼
Knowledge Document
        ▼
Knowledge Chunk
        ▼
Requirement ──► Knowledge Domain
            ──► Citation
            ──► Evaluation Result
```

This relationship model allows multiple documents to support the same requirement and a single document to contribute to multiple domains.

## 3.5 Knowledge Domains

Knowledge Domains divide regulatory information into manageable areas, supporting retrieval, evaluation, coverage measurement, and AI planning.

Domains are **not** determined dynamically by language models. They are defined by the Knowledge Pack and consumed by the Coordinator Agent. This ensures consistency across evaluations and prevents AI models from redefining what constitutes complete regulatory coverage.

## 3.6 Versioning Model

Knowledge Packs are versioned at four independent levels.

| Layer               | Versioned? | Reason                        |
| ------------------- | ---------- | ----------------------------- |
| Source Registry     | ✓          | Government sources evolve     |
| Knowledge Documents | ✓          | Legislation changes           |
| Knowledge Pack      | ✓          | Publication releases          |
| Evaluation Results  | ✓          | Performance changes over time |

Embeddings are **not** considered canonical versions. They are implementation artifacts that may be regenerated without affecting the published Knowledge Pack.

## 3.7 Publication Model

```
Draft → Internal Review → Validation → Evaluation →
Publication Candidate → Published → Maintenance → Superseded → Archived
```

A Knowledge Pack cannot be published until: required authoritative sources are collected · validation succeeds · evaluation thresholds are met · Trust Layer requirements are satisfied · publication metadata is complete.

## 3.8 Architecture Boundaries

A Knowledge Pack deliberately excludes: AI prompts · model parameters · business logic · user interfaces · workflow execution · launch plans · founder data. Those belong to other architectural components. The Knowledge Pack provides structured knowledge only.

## 3.9 Scalability

The architecture must support expansion across countries, industries, regulatory domains, languages, and future AI models. No architectural redesign should be required when adding a new jurisdiction. Scaling is achieved by creating additional Knowledge Packs that conform to this specification.

## 3.10 Success Criteria

A Knowledge Pack architecture is successful when it supports reproducible knowledge acquisition, preserves complete provenance, enables efficient retrieval, supports trustworthy AI reasoning, maintains regulatory traceability, enables quantitative evaluation, and scales across jurisdictions without architectural modification.

# 4. Source Hierarchy & Authority Framework

## 4.1 Purpose

The quality of every Knowledge Pack is determined by the quality of its sources. A retrieval system cannot compensate for unreliable information. Likewise, a highly capable language model cannot transform an untrustworthy source into trustworthy advice.

Accordingly, every source considered for inclusion in a Knowledge Pack shall be evaluated according to a standardized Source Hierarchy and assigned a Source Authority level consistent with the Trust Layer Specification.

## 4.2 Guiding Principle

Authority is determined by **who is responsible for the information**, not by how easy it is to access.

A beautifully designed website operated by an unofficial organisation carries less authority than a poorly formatted government gazette. Ease of retrieval, readability, popularity, and search engine ranking shall never influence Source Authority.

## 4.3 Canonical Source Hierarchy

> **Aligned to ADR-0015 (ratified 2026-08-07) on 2026-08-08.** Source Authority classifies **the document**, never the organisation that published it. See §4.3.1.

**Level 5 — Primary legislation.** Acts of Parliament · Constitutions · Orders in Council · Judicial decisions (where applicable) · Official treaty texts. These instruments create legal obligations directly.

**Level 4 — Statutory instruments and official regulations.** Regulations · Statutory Instruments · Official Gazette notices carrying legal effect. These are made under primary legislation and carry the force of law.

**Level 3 — Official government guidance published by the responsible agency.** Application guides · published fee schedules · official forms · operational manuals · FAQs · ministry and departmental guidance documents. Level 3 sources explain how obligations are fulfilled rather than creating the obligations themselves.

**Level 2 — Government-supported or institutional publication.** Chambers of Commerce · professional institutes · recognised industry associations · university publications · international organisations. Provide useful context but shall not establish regulatory requirements independently.

**Level 1 — Any other source.** News articles · blogs · commercial websites · marketing material · discussion forums · social media. May assist discovery but shall never establish compliance requirements.

### 4.3.1 Authority is a property of the document, not the publisher

**A document does not acquire authority because a ministry or department published it.**

This is the single most important rule in this section. Ministries publish material at several authority levels: an Act they administer is Level 5, a regulation they issue is Level 4, and the guidance page explaining that regulation is Level 3 — even though the same department published all three.

Accordingly:

- **🟢 VERIFIED requires Source Authority ≥ 4** — legislation or regulations only (ADR-0015).
- **Official government guidance (Level 3) can never qualify for VERIFIED**, regardless of which ministry, department, or regulatory authority published it. It may reach 🟡 DERIVED at most.
- There is no authority level for "an organisation". Regulators, licensing agencies, tax authorities and immigration departments are _publishers_; the level is determined by **what they published**.

Authority is assigned at source registration by document type, which makes it objective and reproducible — two researchers classifying the same document must reach the same level.

## 4.4 Source Acceptance Policy

| Authority Level               | Discovery      | Evidence     | Requirement Source | Citation |
| ----------------------------- | -------------- | ------------ | ------------------ | -------- |
| Level 5 — primary legislation | ✓              | ✓            | ✓                  | ✓        |
| Level 4 — regulations / SIs   | ✓              | ✓            | ✓                  | ✓        |
| Level 3 — official guidance   | ✓              | ✓            | Supporting only    | ✓        |
| Level 2 — institutional       | ✓              | Context only | No                 | Optional |
| Level 1 — any other source    | Discovery only | No           | Never              | Never    |

This distinction ensures that low-authority information can help researchers locate authoritative material without contaminating the published Knowledge Pack.

## 4.5 Conflicting Sources

Conflicts between sources shall be resolved according to authority, not recency.

1. Higher Source Authority prevails.
2. If authority is equal, the newer version prevails.
3. If ambiguity remains, both interpretations are retained pending expert review.
4. **No AI-generated interpretation may resolve a legal conflict independently.**

Every unresolved conflict must be explicitly recorded within the Knowledge Pack.

## 4.6 Authority Is Not Evidence

Source Authority and Evidence Strength are independent.

- A Level 5 Act mentioning "business licences" in passing may provide weak evidence for a specific licensing requirement.
- A Level 3 official guidance document describing the exact renewal process may provide stronger _evidence_ for that procedural claim — while remaining a Level 3 _source_, and therefore incapable of supporting a VERIFIED classification on its own.

Accordingly, Source Authority alone shall never determine the Trust Layer outcome. Evidence must still directly support the generated claim.

## 4.7 Source Lifecycle

```
Discovered → Qualified → Authority Assigned → Approved → Acquired →
Version Controlled → Published → Monitored → Superseded → Archived
```

Sources may be withdrawn if their authority changes or if a superior authoritative source becomes available.

## 4.8 Monitoring

Every authoritative source included within a Knowledge Pack must have an associated monitoring strategy: Gazette publication monitoring · RSS feeds · government update services · website change detection · manual legal review · scheduled verification.

Knowledge Packs are living assets. Maintaining source currency is considered part of Knowledge Pack ownership.

## 4.9 Success Criteria

Successful when: every source has an assigned authority level · authority assignment is reproducible · conflicting sources are documented · provenance is preserved · unsupported requirements cannot enter the Knowledge Pack · low-authority sources cannot elevate trust scores independently.

# 5. Knowledge Acquisition Process

## 5.1 Purpose

Defines the standardized methodology by which authoritative information is identified, collected, verified, and prepared for inclusion within a FoundryAI Knowledge Pack.

Knowledge acquisition is considered a controlled engineering process, not a general research activity. Every step must be reproducible.

## 5.2 Guiding Principles

**Completeness before speed.** Researchers shall prioritize discovering all relevant regulatory requirements before optimizing collection efficiency. Incomplete knowledge is considered a more serious defect than slow acquisition.

**Authority before convenience.** Primary legal sources always take precedence over easier-to-access summaries or unofficial guidance.

**Evidence before interpretation.** The platform stores what authoritative sources state. Interpretation occurs only after evidence has been collected.

**Traceability by default.** Every acquired fact must remain traceable back to its originating source throughout the lifecycle of the Knowledge Pack.

**Human-reviewable.** A qualified reviewer must be capable of reproducing every acquisition decision using only the recorded evidence.

**Continuous improvement.** Knowledge acquisition is iterative. Discovery may continue after publication as new authoritative sources become available.

## 5.3 Acquisition Workflow

```
1. Jurisdiction Definition
2. Regulatory Landscape Discovery
3. Source Identification
4. Source Qualification
5. Source Acquisition
6. Metadata Capture
7. Completeness Review
8. Publication Approval
```

Each stage produces documented outputs that become part of the permanent audit trail.

## 5.4 Stage 1 — Jurisdiction Definition

Record: Country · Territory · Region (if applicable) · Government level · Language · Currency · Legal system · Target industries · Regulatory domains · Publication date.

Example:

```
Country:        The Bahamas
Legal System:   Common Law
Target Industry: Restaurants
Domains:        Business Registration, Food Safety, Employment,
                Tax, Environmental Health, Fire Safety, Tourism Licensing
```

No research begins until the jurisdiction has been defined.

## 5.5 Stage 2 — Regulatory Landscape Discovery

The objective is to understand **what regulatory domains exist**, not yet to collect documents. Researchers build an inventory of ministries, departments, licensing bodies, regulators, statutory agencies, local authorities, and courts (where applicable).

Example: Registrar General · Department of Inland Revenue · Department of Environmental Health · National Insurance Board · Ministry of Tourism · Department of Labour · Business Licence Unit · VAT Department.

At this stage no requirements are extracted. The objective is simply to map the ecosystem.

## 5.6 Stage 3 — Source Identification

For every authority identified, researchers locate all available authoritative materials: Acts · Regulations · Gazettes · Licensing guides · Official forms · Application instructions · Fee schedules · Inspection manuals · Official FAQs.

Each discovered source enters the Source Register. No document is considered trusted until qualified.

## 5.7 Stage 4 — Source Qualification

Qualification determines: Source Authority level · publisher · ownership · publication status · jurisdiction · language · publication date · revision history · accessibility · monitoring strategy.

Only qualified sources may progress. Rejected sources remain recorded together with the rejection reason.

## 5.8 Stage 5 — Source Acquisition

Approved sources are collected in their original form. Preferred acquisition order: PDF → HTML → Official API → Machine-readable publication → Scanned document.

Original documents are never modified. Working copies are created separately.

Every acquired document receives: checksum · acquisition timestamp · version identifier · storage location · retrieval method.

## 5.9 Stage 6 — Metadata Capture

Minimum metadata: unique source ID · title · authority level · publisher · jurisdiction · publication date · effective date · version · language · URL · checksum · acquisition date · monitoring method · review status.

Metadata must be machine-readable.

## 5.10 Stage 7 — Completeness Review

The review asks: Have all known regulators been examined? Are all major regulatory domains represented? Are all primary Acts located? Are all supporting Regulations located? Have application processes been collected? Have official fee schedules been collected? Have update mechanisms been identified?

The objective is to identify missing domains before publication. This review contributes to the internal Coverage Confidence assessment defined in the Trust Layer Specification.

## 5.11 Stage 8 — Publication Approval

Approval confirms: acquisition complete · metadata complete · authority assigned · provenance preserved · documents version controlled · monitoring configured · completeness reviewed.

Only after approval may downstream processes begin: Requirement Extraction · Knowledge Chunking · Citation Mapping · Agent Consumption.

## 5.12 Acquisition Artefacts

Jurisdiction Definition Record · Regulatory Landscape Register · Source Register · Acquisition Log · Metadata Catalogue · Completeness Review Checklist · Publication Approval Record.

These artefacts become part of the permanent audit history.

## 5.13 Success Criteria

Successful when: every regulatory domain has been investigated · every accepted source has documented provenance · rejected sources are recorded · metadata is complete · monitoring is configured · acquisition is reproducible · downstream teams can begin extraction without repeating research.

Success is measured by process quality, not by the number of documents collected.

## 5.14 Relationship to Other Specifications

This process precedes: Requirement Extraction Methodology · Knowledge Validation Standard · Knowledge Chunking Strategy · Metadata Standard · Trust Layer Specification. It provides the authoritative inputs required by those processes.
